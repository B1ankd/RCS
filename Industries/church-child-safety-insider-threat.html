The Wolf Has a Name Tag (and a Background Check)

Why abuse in children's ministry almost always comes from someone already trusted inside the church, and the G.U.A.R.D. framework that makes it hard for a trusted person to do harm and easy for anyone to notice if they try.

Read the Article
Home › Houses of Worship › The Wolf Has a Name Tag

As featured in Religious Product News. This article by Chris Orta was originally published in the October 2026 Children's Ministry Safety issue of Religious Product News and is republished here with the publisher's permission.

By Chris Orta, CEO of Red Cell Solutions · Originally published October 2026

Key Takeaways

  • Abuse in a church is rarely a stranger. About eighty percent of offenders hold an official role.
  • Background checks keep out people who have already been caught. They don't stop a trusted insider.
  • The G.U.A.R.D. framework makes it hard for a trusted person to do harm and easy for anyone to notice.
  • Every rule applies to leadership too, including the senior pastor and the elder board.

Every child extends a blind trust to the adults around them. Parents first, and then, by extension, whoever those parents trust. That transfer of trust is the whole system that children's ministry is built on. It is also, tragically, the system abusers rely on.

One in four. Read it again and let it settle in your spirit. Roughly one in four American girls, and one in twenty boys, will experience sexual abuse or assault before they turn eighteen. Not in any one place, but somewhere in the ordinary landscape of their childhood. Those are the same children that attend church with us every Sunday morning.

Abuse in the Church Is Rarely a Stranger

When abuse does occur in a church, it is almost never a stranger who slipped through a door. In the largest study of abuse in American Protestant congregations, about eighty percent of offenders held an official role in the church. Two out of three were pastors or youth ministers, the very people we hand our children to. That is a hard thing to say from inside the church. It is also where the hope is, because this abuse isn't random. And what is not random can be studied and stopped.

What the Corporate World Calls Insider Threat

The corporate world has a name for this problem, and a set of strategies built around it. It's called "insider threat." Insider threat work assumes that an individual has already aced the interview, passed the background checks, and been handed the keys and trust that comes with it. Banks, hospitals, defense contractors, and entire governments have spent decades and enormous sums learning this the hard way. Eventually they stopped asking "How do we keep bad people out?" and instead started asking "How do we make it hard for a trusted person to do harm, and easy for anyone to notice if they try?" That second question is one that many churches, unfortunately, don't know exists, don't know how to tackle, or worse have chosen to ignore.

An offender who is already inside your church has one thing working for them: the belief held by everyone around them, the belief that a person that is trusted could never do harm. You don't find them by guessing who they are. You find them by building a church environment where the people who are harmed can speak, and be believed. The G.U.A.R.D. framework is how you begin to do that.

The G.U.A.R.D. Framework for Children's Ministry Safety

Dr. Anna Salter, a psychologist who has spent four decades interviewing sex offenders, found something most churches get backwards: children often do tell. In case after case, a child had disclosed and simply wasn't believed. The failure was rarely the child's silence but rather the adults who couldn't imagine that someone so trusted could be dangerous. GUARD is built to solve that exact problem. Four of its five parts remove the opportunity to abuse in the first place. The fifth makes sure that when a child does speak, the disclosure lands somewhere it cannot be quietly dismissed.

The G.U.A.R.D. Checklist

  • G: Guard every interaction. Two adults, always: rooms, cars, texts.
  • U: Understand grooming. Train adults to see the precursor behavior. The behavior that they can fall prey to just as much as the child.
  • A: Authorize by task, not title. Define what a role includes and, more importantly, what it doesn't.
  • R: Receive every report, outside the chain of command. There should be somewhere that reports received can't be buried, and where there is logging and accountability from multiple people. Credible concerns go to the proper authorities.
  • D: Declare it to the children themselves. Teach kids, in age-appropriate terms, that no adult is above being questioned, that they can say no, and that telling a trusted grown-up is always right and never gets them in trouble.

Every one of these applies to the senior pastor, the elder board, and the person who wrote the policy. A control that exempts leadership isn't a control.

What One Church Does Better: Accountability Groups

But there is still something better. After working with a number of churches, one stayed with me, not because it had avoided tragedy, but rather because of what it required before anyone could serve. It mirrors, almost exactly, how the corporate world stops insider threats before they form.

The requirement: Accountability groups. That's it. This church doesn't let anyone volunteer with children, or in any other part of ministry, until they have been doing life in one of these accountability groups for at least three years. The groups meet weekly, and people share the real parts of themselves, the temptations and the failures, holding one another accountable to each other and to the Lord. It's James 5:16 being lived out in real time. Confession in the light, where what festers in secret cannot survive. At the staff level, the same groups give leaders a place to name concerns and moral struggles out loud, early.

No one wakes up one day and decides to abuse a child. It is a slow erosion, and abuse in a church is almost always committed by someone deeply embedded in it. Whereas, a community that holds each other accountable is in the rare position to notice the drift while there is still time to act. No system is perfect. But this is the closest I have seen.

Children's Ministry Safety FAQ

Who commits most child abuse in churches?

Usually someone already trusted. In the largest study of abuse in American Protestant congregations, about eighty percent of offenders held an official role in the church, and two out of three were pastors or youth ministers.

Are background checks enough to keep children safe at church?

No. A background check only catches someone who has already been caught. Insider threat thinking assumes the person passed every check, so the protection has to come from how ministry is run: two-adult rules, defined roles, and a reporting path that leadership cannot bury.

What is the G.U.A.R.D. framework?

G.U.A.R.D. is a five-part children's ministry safety framework: Guard every interaction, Understand grooming, Authorize by task not title, Receive every report outside the chain of command, and Declare it to the children themselves. Four parts remove the opportunity to abuse, and the fifth makes sure a child's disclosure cannot be quietly dismissed.

Should child safety rules apply to church leadership?

Yes. Every control applies to the senior pastor, the elder board, and the person who wrote the policy. A control that exempts leadership isn't a control.
